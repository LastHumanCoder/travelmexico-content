A 7-Day Yucatan Road Trip Itinerary

A loop built for one easy week of driving: Merida, Uxmal, Izamal, Valladolid, Chichen Itza and cenotes.

The short answer

A great one-week Yucatan road trip loops from Merida to the Puuc ruins, over to Valladolid via Izamal, and out through Chichen Itza and cenotes. Rent a car, base in Merida for three or four nights and Valladolid for two or three, and drive short hops between towns.

This Yucatan road trip itinerary is a loop you can drive in a week, with two comfortable colonial bases and short day trips out from each. The peninsula is one of the easiest parts of Mexico to self-drive: flat roads, short distances between towns and highways that are quiet compared with central Mexico.

The plan keeps you in Merida first and Valladolid second, so you unpack twice, not seven times. Along the way you see the Puuc route ruins, the yellow town of Izamal, a flamingo lagoon, Chichen Itza before the buses arrive and a cenote most afternoons. It also covers renting a car, tolls, gas and topes. Where costs change, check current rates on arrival; nothing here is a guess.

Before you go: renting a car and driving in the Yucatan

Self-driving is the whole point of this route, so get the car logistics right. Most travelers rent a car at Merida airport or the Cancun airport; picking up and dropping off in different cities is possible but usually costs more, so a loop that returns to your start point is often cheaper. Book a car with full coverage and read what the Mexican insurance includes, because rental desks in Mexico commonly require a local liability policy on top of your booking. Bring the physical credit card used to book and the main driver's license.

Roads between the main towns are good and mostly two lanes. The one toll road you will likely meet is the cuota highway between Merida and Cancun, which passes near Chichen Itza and Valladolid; tolls are cash-friendly and rates change, so carry pesos and check current toll prices before you set off. Fill up at Pemex and other branded stations, keep the tank above half on longer legs, and note that many stations are attended, so a small tip for the attendant is normal. Most importantly, learn to spot topes, the speed bumps that guard the entrance and exit of nearly every town and village. They are often unmarked and can be brutal on a rental, so slow right down whenever you enter a built-up area.

Drive in daylight. Livestock, cyclists, and unlit topes make night driving on rural roads a poor idea, and this itinerary is built around short daytime hops so you never have to. Parking in Merida and Valladolid is easy at hotels with lots or in guarded lots near the centers.

Days 1 and 2: Arrive and settle into Merida

Base yourself in Merida, the Yucatan capital and the natural hub for the western half of this loop. On arrival, collect the rental car, check in, and spend the first evening easing into the pace of the city. The historic center comes alive after dark, with free concerts and dancing in the plazas on many nights and the Plaza Grande buzzing with families.

Give Merida a full second day. Walk the grand Paseo de Montejo with its mansions from the henequen boom, visit the cathedral and the Plaza Grande, browse the Lucas de Galvez market, and duck into a museum or two out of the midday heat. There are plenty of things to do in merida mexico beyond the center, including a nearby hacienda or a cenote on the city's edge for an afternoon swim. For a full breakdown of neighborhoods, food and timing, see the Merida guide. Merida also has the region's best restaurant scene, so plan at least one long dinner of Yucatecan cooking like cochinita pibil and sopa de lima.

Day 3: Uxmal and the Puuc route

Drive south for one of the great archaeological days in Mexico. Uxmal is about an hour and a half from Merida and, for many visitors, rivals Chichen Itza while drawing far fewer crowds. Its Pyramid of the Magician and the elegant Governor's Palace show off the ornate Puuc architectural style. Arrive when the site opens to beat the heat, and bring water, a hat and sun protection because shade is limited.

If you have energy, continue along the Puuc route to smaller sites like Kabah before looping back to Merida for the night. This is exactly why the itinerary keeps Merida as a base: you return to the same comfortable hotel instead of packing up. A cenote near the road makes a perfect cool-down on the drive home.

Day 4: Celestun flamingos or the Progreso coast

Choose your coast for day four, then return to Merida. West of the city, Celestun is a biosphere reserve famous for its flamingos; boat tours run into the mangroves and lagoon where the birds gather, with the biggest flocks usually in the cooler months. It is roughly an hour and a half each way, so start early.

If you would rather have a relaxed beach afternoon, drive north instead to Progreso, Merida's nearest Gulf beach town, about forty minutes away, with a long malecon, seafood palapas and calm shallow water. Either way you are back in Merida by evening. This flexibility is part of a good yucatan road trip itinerary: read the weather and your energy, and pick the day trip that fits.

Day 5: Drive to Valladolid via Izamal

Time to shift bases. Rather than take the fast toll road straight through, drive the free road east and stop in Izamal, the Pueblo Magico painted almost entirely in golden yellow. It sits roughly halfway between Merida and Valladolid and makes an ideal mid-morning break. Wander the huge convent, climb for a rooftop view, and take a horse-drawn carriage around the yellow streets before lunch.

From Izamal it is another hour or so to Valladolid, a smaller and slower colonial city that becomes your base for the eastern half of the loop. Check in, then spend the late afternoon on the main square and the pretty Calzada de los Frailes. There are more low-key things to do in valladolid mexico than most people expect, including a cenote right in town. Izamal is one of several pueblos magicos yucatan is known for, and it sets the tone for the more relaxed second half of the trip.

Day 6: Chichen Itza early, cenotes after

This is the day the whole itinerary is designed around. Chichen Itza is only about forty-five minutes from Valladolid, which lets you arrive close to opening, well before the tour buses roll in from Cancun and the Riviera Maya. The great pyramid of El Castillo, the ball court and the observatory are worth the early alarm, and the light and temperature are far kinder first thing. Buy your entry on arrival or online and check current opening hours before you go, since they shift seasonally. For planning the visit in detail, read the Chichen Itza guide.

By late morning, when the site is crowded and hot, you are ready to leave. Spend the afternoon at one or two of the best cenotes in yucatan clustered around Valladolid and the village of Dzitnup, where you can swim in cool, clear water inside limestone caverns. It is the perfect counterpoint to a morning among the ruins, and it is why basing in Valladolid beats day-tripping Chichen Itza from the coast.

Day 7: Valladolid, a final cenote, then onward

Use your last morning for the parts of Valladolid you have not seen: the market, a museum, a slow breakfast, and Cenote Zaci or another swimming spot right in or near town. Then point the car toward your onward destination. Many travelers finish this loop by driving on to Cancun or Tulum on the Caribbean coast, roughly two hours away, to fly home or continue their trip; others return to Merida to close the loop where they started.

Whichever way you end it, you will have driven a complete circuit of the classic inland Yucatan in seven days without ever feeling rushed. For more swimming options along the way, our roundup of the best cenotes in Yucatan pairs naturally with this route.

Before you drive

Do I really need a rental car for this itinerary?

Yes, a car is what makes this loop work. Buses and colectivos connect the main towns, but they will not get you efficiently to Uxmal, the Puuc route, Celestun or the scattered cenotes on your own schedule. A rental car lets you arrive at ruins early and reach swimming spots that tours skip.

Which direction should I drive the loop?

Either works. This plan runs Merida first then Valladolid, which is convenient if you fly into Merida and out of Cancun. If you fly into Cancun, simply reverse it: start in Valladolid, do Chichen Itza early, then move west to Merida for the Puuc route and coast.

Is it enough time, or should I add days?

Seven days is comfortable for the highlights. With more time, add nights in Merida for the Puuc route and a hacienda, or a night on the coast. The itinerary is built so you can drop the Celestun or Progreso day if you are short on time without breaking the loop.

When is the best time to drive the Yucatan?

The cooler, drier months from roughly late fall to early spring are the most comfortable for combining ruins and cenotes, and flamingo numbers at Celestun are often higher then. Summer is hotter and more humid, and the early fall can bring rain, so start ruin visits early whenever you go.
