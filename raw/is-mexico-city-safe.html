Is Mexico City Safe? A Neighborhood-by-Neighborhood Guide

An honest look at where to stay, where to be careful, and how to travel smart in the capital.

The short answer

For most tourists, Mexico City is safe if you use normal big-city common sense. The vast majority of visits are trouble-free. Petty theft and scams exist, so stay in central neighborhoods like Roma, Condesa or Polanco, use registered taxis or Uber, and stay alert.

Is Mexico City safe? For the average traveler sticking to the areas visitors actually use, the honest answer is yes, with the same street sense you would bring to any large capital. The question "is Mexico City safe" comes up constantly because the country makes headlines, but those headlines rarely describe the leafy cafes of Condesa or the museum crowds of the Centro Historico. Millions of people visit each year and go home with nothing worse than a sunburn and a taste for good tacos. When people ask is Mexico City safe for a first trip, the real answer is that most trouble is petty and avoidable.

That does not mean you switch off your instincts. Is Mexico City safe everywhere, at every hour, for everyone? No, and no honest guide would claim so. Pickpocketing on packed metro cars, phone snatching, ATM skimming and the occasional taxi scam are the realistic risks, not the dramatic ones. This guide goes neighborhood by neighborhood so you know where to stay in Mexico City, which areas reward a daytime visit, and which spots call for more caution after dark. We also cover the practical safety habits that matter most, and how the capital compares when travelers ask about the safest places in Mexico. Before any trip, check your own government travel advisory for the latest guidance.

Neighborhood by neighborhood

Where you base yourself shapes your whole experience, so this is the part worth reading closely. The good news is that the best neighborhoods in Mexico City for tourists are also among the most comfortable to walk. Here is how the main visitor areas actually feel, and what each one is good for.

Roma and Condesa

These adjoining neighborhoods are where a lot of first-time visitors choose to stay, and for good reason. Tree-lined streets, independent coffee shops, design stores, parks and a relaxed pace make them easy to settle into. They feel comfortable to walk by day and stay busy into the evening thanks to their restaurants and bars. Use normal caution late at night, keep your phone in your pocket rather than in your hand on the sidewalk, and you will find these among the most pleasant places to be based.

Polanco

Polanco is the capital's upscale district, home to major museums, luxury hotels, high-end shopping and some of the country's best-known restaurants. It is polished, well-kept and generally very comfortable for visitors who want a quieter, more residential base. It sits a little apart from the nightlife energy of Roma and Condesa, which some travelers prefer.

Centro Historico

The historic center is the beating heart of the city and unmissable, with the Zocalo, the Metropolitan Cathedral, the Templo Mayor ruins and the Palacio de Bellas Artes all within walking distance. It is lively and heavily touristed by day, which also means crowds, and crowds are where pickpockets work. Keep bags zipped and in front of you, stay to the main streets, and be more deliberate about where you wander once shops close and the area empties out in the evening. Many travelers visit the Centro by day and sleep elsewhere.

Coyoacan

South of the center, Coyoacan is a charming, village-like neighborhood of cobbled streets, plazas, markets and the Frida Kahlo Museum. It has a calm, local feel and is a lovely place to spend a day, or to stay if you want something gentler than the central buzz.

Areas to treat with more care

As in any large city, some districts are primarily residential or industrial and simply are not set up for tourists, and a few have a rougher reputation, especially after dark. Neighborhoods like Tepito and Doctores are commonly cited by locals as places casual visitors have little reason to go. The practical rule is simple: stick to the visitor-friendly areas above, avoid wandering into unfamiliar districts late at night, and if a street suddenly feels quiet and off the tourist path, turn back toward the busier blocks. You do not need to memorize a map of no-go zones, you just need to stay where the visitors are.

Practical Mexico City travel tips

Beyond choosing the right neighborhood, a handful of everyday habits do most of the work of keeping a trip smooth. None of these are dramatic, they are just the small routines experienced travelers follow without thinking.

Taxis and getting around

Do not flag down random street taxis. Use Uber or another ride-hailing app, or an official sitio taxi booked from a stand or through your hotel. App rides give you a record of the driver and route, which is exactly the reassurance you want. The Metrobus and metro are cheap and efficient, but they get very crowded at rush hour, which is prime pickpocket territory, so keep your bag zipped and your phone put away when you ride.

ATMs and money

Use ATMs inside banks, shopping malls or your hotel rather than isolated machines on the street, where skimming devices are more of a risk. Withdraw during the day, cover the keypad, and carry only the cash and one card you need for the day. Leave spare cards and your passport in the hotel safe. Card payment is widely accepted in restaurants and shops in the tourist areas.

Phones and valuables

Opportunistic phone snatching is the most common annoyance, so avoid walking down the sidewalk with your phone out or scrolling at a stoplight. Leave expensive jewelry and flashy watches at home, keep your bag in front of you in crowds, and stay aware in busy markets and on packed transport. A small cross-body bag beats a back pocket every time.

Altitude and staying well

Mexico City sits at roughly 7,350 feet, and the altitude is real. Many visitors feel a little short of breath, headachy or tired on the first day. Take it easy at first, drink plenty of water, go easy on alcohol the first night, and give yourself time before any strenuous plans. For water, stick to bottled or filtered water for drinking, which most hotels and restaurants provide, rather than tap.

Common scams to sidestep

The usual ones are minor: overpriced or rigged unofficial taxis, distraction tactics in crowds, and occasional card issues, which is why you keep the taxi app and the in-bank ATM habits above. Agree on plans and prices before you commit, and be politely firm with anyone unusually pushy on the street. Trust your gut. If something feels engineered, walk on.

Good to know

Is it safe to walk around Mexico City at night?

In central, well-populated neighborhoods like Roma, Condesa and Polanco, walking in the evening is common and generally comfortable, especially on busy streets. Use the same caution you would in any big city late at night: stick to well-lit main streets, keep your phone away, and take an Uber or official taxi for longer distances or when an area feels quiet.

Which neighborhoods are best for tourists to stay in?

Roma and Condesa are the popular walkable favorites, Polanco suits travelers wanting an upscale and quieter base, and Coyoacan offers a calmer, village-like feel. The Centro Historico is great for sightseeing but many visitors prefer to sleep in the other areas and visit the center by day.

Is the tap water safe to drink?

Most visitors stick to bottled or filtered water for drinking, which hotels and restaurants widely provide. Using bottled water and ice from reputable places is the simple, standard precaution.

Should I check a travel advisory before going?

Yes. Advisories and local conditions can change, so review your own government's current travel advisory for Mexico City before you book and again close to departure for the most up-to-date guidance.
