Guadalajara: The Complete Destination Guide

The capital of Jalisco and the birthplace of mariachi and tequila, planned the way a specialist would do it.

The short answer

The best things to do in Guadalajara are exploring the Centro Historico around the Cathedral, seeing the Orozco murals at Hospicio Cabanas, eating birria and tortas ahogadas, and browsing the artisan towns of Tlaquepaque and Tonala. Save a day for the town of Tequila.

Guadalajara is Mexico's second-largest city and the capital of Jalisco, the state that gave the world mariachi, tequila and the charro tradition. It wears all of that lightly. What you get is grand colonial plazas, Orozco's murals, some of the best street food in the country and two craft towns within the city limits.

Fly into Guadalajara's international airport (GDL), 30 to 45 minutes from the center, and give the city three or four days. The things to do in Guadalajara sit close together: the historic core, the food, and the artisan suburbs of Tlaquepaque and Tonala are minutes apart, and the town of Tequila is an hour away. Slow down. The city rewards walking and long coffees more than a checklist.

Things to do in the Centro Historico

Start in the historic center, where the city's monuments cluster around a string of connected plazas. The twin-spired Guadalajara Cathedral is the anchor, its neo-Gothic yellow towers visible from across downtown and flanked by the Plaza de Armas and the leafy Plaza de la Liberacion. Around these squares you will find the Teatro Degollado, a grand nineteenth-century opera house, and the Rotonda de los Jaliscienses Ilustres, a rotunda honoring the state's notable figures.

The single most important cultural stop is the Hospicio Cabanas, a former hospital and orphanage that is now a UNESCO World Heritage site. Its main chapel holds the celebrated murals of Jose Clemente Orozco, including the fiery El Hombre de Fuego on the dome overhead, best viewed lying back on the benches provided. Nearby, the vast Mercado San Juan de Dios (also called Mercado Libertad) is one of the largest indoor markets in Latin America, stacked with food stalls, leather, crafts, and everyday goods. It is a full sensory experience and a good place to try local snacks.

More to see around the city

Beyond the core, wander the tree-lined Colonia Americana, repeatedly named one of the world's coolest neighborhoods for its Porfiriato mansions, cafes, and nightlife. Music lovers should make time for Plaza de los Mariachis, the traditional spot to hear live mariachi, and design-minded travelers will enjoy the boutiques and galleries scattered through Lafayette and Chapultepec. Families often add the Zoologico Guadalajara or a stroll through Parque Agua Azul.

Guadalajara food guide

No Guadalajara food guide is complete without its two signature dishes. Birria, a rich, slow-cooked stew traditionally made with goat and now often with beef, is a Jalisco institution, served with consomme for dipping and increasingly famous abroad as birria tacos. The other local hero is the torta ahogada, a crusty birote roll stuffed with pork and drowned in a bright, spicy tomato and chile de arbol sauce. Eat it with a stack of napkins and no dignity; that is the correct way.

Beyond those, look for tejuino, a cold fermented corn drink sold from street carts, and the classic cantinas of the center where botanas arrive with your drink. Tequila and its smokier cousin from the highlands appear everywhere, naturally, given the region. For dessert, seek out jericalla, a Guadalajara custard similar to flan but with a lightly torched top. We usually tell travelers to eat where the lines form and to make the Mercado San Juan de Dios and the neighborhood markets part of the plan.

Tlaquepaque and Tonala: the artisan towns

Two of the most rewarding half-days in the area are the craft suburbs. Tlaquepaque, now folded into the greater Guadalajara metropolitan area, is a polished, pedestrian-friendly town of colonial facades, art galleries, ceramics studios, and courtyard restaurants. Its central El Parian is a large arcaded plaza ringed with cantinas where mariachi plays, and the main streets are lined with high-quality pottery, blown glass, and papel picado. It is the place to shop for a special piece and to sit for a long lunch.

Nearby Tonala is the workshop to Tlaquepaque's showroom. This is where much of the region's pottery and glass is made, and its street market, held on Thursdays and Sundays, is enormous and far less polished, which is the appeal. Prices tend to be lower and the selection is vast, from hand-painted ceramics to wrought iron. Together, Tlaquepaque and Tonala give you the full arc of Jalisco craft, from the finished gallery piece to the artisan's own stall.

Guadalajara day trips

The two classic Guadalajara day trips are Tequila and Lake Chapala, and both are easy to reach. The town of Tequila sits about an hour to the west, surrounded by the blue agave fields that earn the landscape its UNESCO World Heritage listing. You can tour a distillery to see how the spirit is made from agave heart to bottle, taste along the way, and ride the scenic route out through the fields; several tours and the tourist train run from Guadalajara, so this works well without a car.

Lake Chapala, Mexico's largest natural lake, lies about an hour south and is a favorite escape for a slow lunch by the water. The lakeside towns of Chapala and Ajijic have long drawn a large international and expat community, and Ajijic in particular is known for its cobbled streets, murals, and gentle climate. For something more active, the highland town of Mazamitla and the pine forests to the south make a cooler, greener contrast to the city, though they are better as a longer outing.

Where to stay in Guadalajara

Deciding where to stay in Guadalajara comes down to the kind of trip you want. The Centro Historico puts you within walking distance of the Cathedral, the museums, and the markets, which suits first-time visitors who want the monuments at their doorstep. For a more contemporary, walkable base with the best cafes, restaurants, and nightlife, the Colonia Americana and the neighboring Lafayette and Chapultepec areas are hard to beat and are our usual recommendation for couples.

Travelers focused on shopping and a quieter, boutique feel often prefer to stay in Tlaquepaque, which functions almost like a charming small town within the city. Business travelers and those who want modern hotels tend toward the Zona Financiera around Avenida Lopez Mateos and Andares. Whichever area you choose, the city's ride-hailing and taxi coverage makes it simple to move between neighborhoods, so you are never locked out of the rest of Guadalajara. If you plan to continue to the Pacific coast afterward, the surf town of Sayulita pairs naturally with Jalisco, and many travelers combine the region with a stay in Mexico City.

Best time to visit Guadalajara

The best time to visit Guadalajara is the dry season, which runs roughly from November through May. Sitting on the central highland plateau, the city enjoys a mild, spring-like climate for much of the year, with warm days and cool evenings. The rains arrive mainly from June through October, usually as dramatic late-afternoon downpours that clear quickly and leave the surrounding countryside lush and green, so summer travel is still very doable if you plan around the afternoons.

For festivals, Guadalajara shines in the fall. The city hosts the Encuentro Internacional del Mariachi in late August and September and the huge Fiestas de Octubre through October, while the Guadalajara International Book Fair, one of the largest in the world, fills late November and early December. If you want reliable sun and outdoor plazas, aim for the dry months; if you come for the culture calendar, the shoulder season around the rains can be the most rewarding time of all.

Good to know

How many days do you need in Guadalajara?

Three to four days is a comfortable amount of time. That lets you cover the Centro Historico and Hospicio Cabanas, eat your way through the food scene, spend a half-day in Tlaquepaque and Tonala, and still take one day trip to Tequila or Lake Chapala.

Is Guadalajara easy to get around without a car?

Yes. The historic center is very walkable, and ride-hailing apps and taxis are widely available and affordable for reaching Tlaquepaque, Tonala, and the newer neighborhoods. For day trips to Tequila you can join a tour or take the tourist train, so a rental car is optional.

What food is Guadalajara known for?

Birria, a rich slow-cooked stew, and the torta ahogada, a pork sandwich drowned in spicy sauce, are the signatures. Also look for tejuino, a fermented corn drink, and jericalla, a local torched custard. Tequila from the nearby region is, of course, everywhere.

Is Tequila worth a day trip from Guadalajara?

For most travelers, yes. The town sits amid the blue agave fields about an hour away, you can tour a distillery to see how tequila is made, and the landscape is a UNESCO World Heritage site. It is one of the most popular and easiest day trips from the city.
